I. INTRODUCTION

Competitive programming (CP) has become one of the more reliable extra-curricular routes for building algorithmic reasoning in computer science students [1], [2]. Codeforces, LeetCode, and AtCoder all supply the same basic ingredients: immediate verdicts, timed contests, and a public leaderboard against which progress can be measured [3]. What separates this from a graded laboratory assignment is the absence of scaffolding. Problems arrive without hints, under fixed time and memory budgets, and the student has to decide on their own what to try next. Employers value exactly that ability [4].

The difficulty is staying with it. A large share of undergraduates begin with real enthusiasm and stop within months months, a pattern also documented for dropout from introductory programming courses [5]. Computing education research has linked this pattern to swings in programming self-efficacy, a sense that progress has stalled, social isolation, and pressure from coursework [6], [7]. Dropout itself is well trodden ground in educational data mining: institutional models have been built on grade records, attendance, and clickstreams from massive open online courses [8], [9]. Competitive programming sits outside all of that. It happens off the transcript, runs on voluntary effort, and has attracted very little empirical attention [10].

Our motivation is practical. Programming clubs and faculty mentors have no systematic way of noticing that a student is drifting until the student has gone. If that drift could be detected while the student is still submitting, there would be room to act: a conversation, a pairing with a senior member, a structured review session after the next contest.

We therefore built a two-layer study and framed it around three questions. RQ1 asks which behavioral and academic reasons students themselves give select, from a closed list, for discontinuing competitive programming. RQ2 asks whether behavioral proxies drawn from Codeforces activity separate active users from true attrition users. users once the two groups are matched on peak rating. RQ3 asks whether models trained on Codeforces behavior or on psychographic survey responses can pick out at-risk individuals among students who are still active. identify attrition better than trivial baselines.

The paper makes four contributions. First, we identify build a rating-matched Codeforces cohort with a documented stage-by-stage flow and test the Skill-Application Paradox, in which students who quit report at least as much theoretical confidence as students who stay while practising noticeably less. an explicit data-quality rule. Second, we introduce two domain-specific interaction features, the Intensity Ratio and the Editorial Dependency Index. Index, and report how they behave once temporal leakage is removed. Third, we establish benchmark nine classifiers against trivial baselines with per-fold uncertainty and a permutation test. In doing so, we quantify how much apparent signal in this kind of study comes from skill confounding, timing leakage and incomplete platform histories. Fourth, we report an empirical baseline linking global telemetry exploratory survey analysis restricted to localized survey data, items that are comparable across questionnaire branches, together with the pipeline described in enough detail to be reproduced. Fourth, we deploy an Early Warning System over currently active students as a proof of concept, scoring every student out of fold. full instrument, data and code.

II. RELATED WORK

A. Educational Data Mining and Student Dropout

Work on student retention has shifted over two decades. Early models predicted degree completion from demographics and first-year marks [8], [11]. Attention later moved to dynamic behavior, submission patterns and forum participation, driven by the data online courses made available [9]. Random Forest [12], XGBoost [13] and LightGBM [14] are now the default comparison points for tabular educational prediction . prediction.

B. Programming Platform Telemetry

Studies of online judges show that granular submission logs carry information about cognitive state: compilation error frequency, inter-submission gaps and wrong-answer rates have been treated as proxies for frustration and learning difficulty [10], [15]. Cognitive load theory gives a reason to take long struggle times seriously, since past a point repeated failure signals overload rather than productive effort [16]. Most of this work sits in structured assignments with a fixed deadline; voluntary competitive participation behaves differently, and disengagement plays out over weeks.

C. Psychographic and Social Factors

Motivational orientation and peer support are established predictors of persistence in computing [17], [18]. Competitive environments complicate the picture. Adoption of programming contests has been studied as a diffusion process driven by peer influence and perceived benefit [19], which implies the same social channels can work in reverse when progress stalls. Educational data mining on introductory programming has also shown that behavioral traces predict failure earlier than static background variables [20]; our design follows that logic into the voluntary setting.

D. The Integration Gap

We are not aware of any prior study pairing competitive programming platform logs with psychographic survey responses. Telemetry is objective but silent on why a user stopped; surveys reach the reasoning yet depend on recall recall, and both raise questions of student agency and data ethics [22]. We use one to compensate for In this study the other. two sources share no subjects, features or labels, so we report them side by side rather than combining them.

Twelve features were derived from the raw API responses. Six are direct behavioral proxies: math solve rate, data structure solve rate, upsolves per contest, mean struggle minutes, activity trend ratio, and post-drop recovery. Two further features are interactions we constructed for this problem. The Intensity Ratio asks how much solving a user does relative to how often they compete:

IR_i IR_(i) = P_month,i P_(month,i) / (C_month,i (C_(month,i) + e) (1) (1)

where P_month is problems solved per month, C_month is contests entered per month, and e = 0.1 guards the denominator against users with no recent contest activity. A high value means the user is solving a great deal relative to how much they compete, which is the pattern we associate with self-directed practice. Because both monthly rates share the same denominator, the length of the user's submission span, that span cancels and IR approximately equals problems solved per rated contest. The audited specification therefore uses the exactly window-invariant form SPC_(i) = S_(i)/C_(i), where S_(i) is distinct problems solved and C_(i) rated contests (Spearman ρ = 0.989 with IR).

The Editorial Dependency Index encodes a suspicion we had going in, that attrition-bound users upsolve often but without much independent effort:

EDI_i EDI_(i) = U_i x dT_struggle,i (2) U_(i) × ΔT_(struggle,i) (2)

where U_i U_(i) is upsolving frequency per contest (accepted practice-mode submissions to contests the user entered) and dT_struggle ΔT_(struggle) is the mean time to acceptance, itself defined as the mean of T_AC T_(AC) minus T_first T_(first) over all accepted problems. A high EDI sitting next to low contest frequency and a low Intensity Ratio describes a student who reads is the solution after every round and is not otherwise building capacity. pattern the index was designed to detect.

Decay in activity is captured by the Activity Trend Ratio, Ratio: the ratio number of submissions in the recent three months to those second half of the user's own submission span divided by the number in the prior three, with anything under 1.0 indicating a slowdown. first half, set to 1 when the first half is empty. An account is flagged for activity decay when this ratio falls below 0.5. Three columns were deliberately kept out: months since last submission encodes the label definition directly, peak rating appears in the label threshold, and current rating is not measured at a comparable point in time across the two groups.

V. DISCUSSION

A. What the Two Layers Say Together

We described this work in an earlier draft as a framework in which the two layers converge. That word claims more than the design supports, and we withdraw it. The layers share no respondents, no features and no labels. The Codeforces cohort is a global sample of anonymous accounts labeled by an inactivity proxy; the survey cohort is 64 students at Bangladeshi universities labeled by self-report. Nothing links an individual in one to an individual in the other, and no statistic computed on one constrains a statistic computed on the other.

What we can claim is weaker and worth stating precisely. Two independent studies, on disjoint populations with disjoint instruments, return findings that The two analyses also do not point the same way. Platform data shows disengagement arriving after contest participation falls. Survey data shows In the platform data, users who withdrew were slightly less accurate and less active than matched peers. In the survey, students who stopped practising least consistently between contests, with rated their own ability slightly higher, without a significant difference. The upsolving contrast that the one feature that separates published version took as a shared direction rested on non-comparable survey items. Self-ratings and platform accuracy measure different things, and the groups under correction. That both point to practice frequency between contests survey comparison is a replication of direction, not a joint result, and its evidential value lies exactly in retrospective for the independence of stopped group. We therefore treat the two measurements: a bias in the Codeforces inactivity proxy has no route to the survey, and recall bias among respondents who stopped has no route to the platform logs. analyses as separate pieces of evidence.

B. Practical Implications

If Declining contest and practice frequency is the most consistent behavioral difference we observe, and a university programming club wanted to act on one thing from this study, we would suggest upsolving discipline. Our results do not, however, support automated risk scoring of individual students, and checking the effect of such interventions would need to be tested in on students returning from a long break would. controlled study.

C. Limitations

Several things constrain what we can claim. The survey modeling set is small at 64 64, and it is confined to Bangladeshi universities, so generalization is untested. The Codeforces labels rest on an inactivity proxy that cannot separate a permanent departure from a semester spent on coursework. The survey model's corrected F1 still likely overstates what it would achieve elsewhere. coursework, and the peak-rating cut-off at 1500 is a study choice. Features summarize each account's history up to T_(obs) rather than a fixed window before the stop. A fixed-window rebuild from the raw API records is provided with the deposited code. Codeforces users are a global population, not specifically university students.

There is also a bias we cannot design away. Features such as upsolving habit data structure understanding, mentor support and prior consideration of quitting were reported by students who had already stopped, which means we are asking people to recall their past behavior from inside their current disengagement. If that colors their answers systematically, the separation we observe between the cohorts on self-reported measures is inflated to an unknown degree.

Finally, the Early Warning System run over 22 students is a demonstration and nothing more. We have no follow-up data confirming whether questionnaire branches asked different questions, which limits the six flagged students subsequently disengaged or their performance declined. between-group comparisons that are possible. Both datasets are observational throughout, so nothing reported here supports a causal claim.

VI. CONCLUSION

This paper set out to study attrition in competitive programming using two independent sources: Codeforces behavioral logs after filtering, data-quality checks, rating matching and balancing, and a an anonymous student survey across ten universities in Bangladesh. The behavioral layer confirmed that attrition follows an 83.7 percent collapse in contest participation In the rating-matched cohort, users who withdrew had entered fewer rated contests, solved fewer problems, and consistent underperformance across skill-linked metrics, were slightly less accurate on math- and these data-structure-tagged problems. These differences persist when the two cohorts are held to the same peak-rating ceiling. significant after correction but small.

The Skill-Application Paradox held up asymmetrically. is not established. Its behavioral half is firm: upsolving habit separates the cohorts at p < 0.001 with a large effect, surviving correction for eight tests, and it is the dominant feature in the rested on survey model at 35.5 percent. items that cannot be compared across branches. Its confidence half is directional only, with only: the mathematics self-rating gap is nominal at p = 0.027 and does not surviving survive correction.

On the modeling side, Random Forest performed window-invariant behavioral features predict attrition above chance but modestly, with a best on both layers: 0.682 on repeated-CV ROC-AUC of 0.622. No model exceeds the audited behavioral specification F1 of a trivial baseline, and 0.832 on the survey layer, where regularized logistic regression matched it. We offer these results as an exploratory baseline rather than a finished system. The work we most want to do next is longitudinal: larger cohorts followed across universities and academic years, repository activity and club attendance folded into the feature set, and a prospective trial of the Early Warning System prospective, validated early-warning procedure developed with a partner programming club. A second priority is qualitative. RQ1 asks why students leave, and a closed six-option item cannot answer that; semi-structured interviews with a subset of the cohort that stopped, coded under a stated procedure with inter-coder reliability reported, would replace the frequency distribution we offer here with something that genuinely addresses the question.

APPENDIX B: SURVEY INSTRUMENT

The questionnaire opens with six items put to every respondent, then branches on Q6. Items marked with an asterisk enter the feature set. Response options are given where the item is closed.

A. Common Items (All Respondents)

Q1 current semester. Q2 current CGPA. Q3* programming experience before university (yes / no). Q4* grade in the Data Structures and Algorithms course (A+ or A / B+ or B / C+ or C / D or below / not yet taken). Q5* self-rated mathematics ability (1 to 5). Q6 current CP status (currently active / previously active but stopped / never started), which determines the branch.

B. Active Branch (Q7 to Q29, n = 22)

Q7 hours per week on CP. Q8 platforms used. Q9 current rating. Q10 contests entered per month. Q11* upsolving after contests (always / sometimes / rarely / never). Q12 new problems solved per month. Q13 time on a problem before opening the editorial. Q14* understanding of standard data structures (1 to 5). Q15 comfort with dynamic programming and graph problems. Q16 language used. Q17 mental state when rating falls. Q18 frequency of burnout. Q19 demotivation from comparing ratings. Q20 motivation for doing CP. Q21 whether fear of failure deters contest entry. Q22* access to a mentor or senior (yes / no). Q23 quality of club support. Q24 internet or hardware constraints. Q25 effect of academic pressure. Q26 family pressure toward grades. Q27* any break of 15 or more consecutive days in the last three months (yes / no). Q28* ever seriously considered quitting (yes / no). Q29 expectation of still being active in six months.

C. Stopped Branch (Q30 to Q39, n = 51)

Q30 duration of practice before stopping. Q31 main reason for stopping, a single selection from: rating not increasing or no progress; lack of guidance or mentor; academic pressure with CGPA focus; mental stress or burnout; interest in other tracks such as web development or machine learning; others. Q32 highest rating before stopping. Q33* practice habit in the final month (regular / became irregular / stopped completely). Q34* ever seriously considered quitting (yes / no). Q35* had mentor or senior support (yes / no). Q36* any break of 15 or more consecutive days during the CP journey (yes / no). Q37* understanding of standard data structures at the point of stopping (1 to 5). Q38 whether stopping was foreseeable six months earlier. Q39 peers in the friend circle doing CP.

D. Never-Started Branch (Q40 to Q49, n = 23)

Excluded from all modeling and all tests reported here.

E. Branch Alignment

Q11 Q3, Q4 and Q33 Q5 are mapped common to a common four-level scale: regular maps to sometimes, became irregular to rarely, and stopped completely to never. all respondents. Q14 pairs with Q37, Q22 with Q35, Q27 with Q36, and Q28 with Q34. Q34, each with parallel wording. Q11 and Q33 measure different behaviors and Q27 and Q36 refer to different periods, so neither pair is aligned. Q31 is used for label refinement, with interest in other tracks classifying an intentional exit. Q39 has no active-branch counterpart and is therefore excluded from both the statistical tests and the model. counterpart. Items that are not aligned are reported within their branch only.
