ITEM 1.0  AN ADDITIONAL PROBLEM WE FOUND OURSELVES
====================================================================================================
(Reported first because it bears on Items 1.1-1.3.)
While rebuilding the cohort we checked every account for internal consistency. Every rated contest
requires at least one submission, yet 774 accounts in the released file had fewer retrieved
submissions than rated contests (a median of 6 submissions against 24 rated contests, all dated
within the month before collection). A further 114 accounts showed more than 15 rated contests per
month of submission span; the highest value among inactive accounts was 9. All 888 of these accounts
were active, and 880 remain after the published peak-rating filter. Their retrieved histories are
incomplete, so their submission-derived features are not reliable. In the published unmatched
sample, 148 of them raised the active mean contest rate to 7.27 per month; without them the mean is
1.85. The published "83.7 percent fall in contest participation" was therefore largely an artifact
of incomplete data.

What we changed. Such accounts are excluded by an explicit rule (Dataset 1 subsection, new data-
quality paragraph; Table I). The primary model is also reported without the rule, so the Desk can
see the rule's effect (best AUC 0.663 without it, 0.622 with it). The raw records allow these
accounts to be checked directly.

1.1  Specification A and values identical to the unmatched preprint
-------------------------------------------------------------------

INQUIRY (verbatim): "The rating-matched analysis (Specification A) reports descriptive statistics,
p-values, feature importances, and proportions identical to your earlier unmatched preprint, raising
questions about whether you performed the described matching procedure."

What we found. We respectfully clarify one point of reading and acknowledge one error. The
clarification is that in the published Table III, Specification (A) is labelled "Unmatched". It was
included on purpose, as the paper says, "so the effect of the confound is visible", and is therefore
identical to the preprint by design. The rating-matched specifications are (B) and (C). They were
computed on the matched cohort, and the deposited script r1 reproduces them: for (C), logistic
regression 0.639, KNN 0.589, SVM 0.652, XGBoost 0.652, LightGBM 0.645, MLP 0.608 and the ensemble
0.679 are identical to the published values. Random Forest (0.682 published) and the Decision Tree
differ only in the third decimal across library versions. The published Table I is also reproduced
exactly from the data: 6,927, then 6,892, then 5,573 and 1,319, then 908 and 411, with a matching
pool of 4,047.

The error is that the descriptive part of the paper was not recomputed on the matched cohort. The
subsection "Behavioral Trends Preceding Attrition" and Figs. 4-8 are the first-submission results on
the unmatched cohort, and the five figure files are byte-identical to those of the first submission.
This includes 7.27 vs. 1.19 contests per month (83.7 percent), 2,646 vs. 3,059 minutes (15.6
percent), 13.70 vs. 7.86 problems per month (42.7 percent), burnout of 45.0 vs. 29.8 percent, and
upsolving of 2.94 vs. 2.81. The abstract and conclusion repeated these figures. The conclusion also
stated that "these differences persist when the two cohorts are held to the same peak-rating
ceiling", which we had not computed. The feature importances in Fig. 14 are the unaudited ones, as
their caption states.

What we changed. Every descriptive statistic, test and proportion is now computed on the rating-
matched cohort (first Results subsection, new Table III, Figs. 2-3), and the matched cohort is
deposited. The following values show that matching changes the results:
  - Peak rating: standardized mean difference 0.66 unmatched -> 0.055 matched; Kolmogorov-Smirnov D
    0.282 -> 0.061 (p = 0.071).
  - Contests per month: published (unmatched means) 7.27 vs 1.19; corrected (matched medians, active
    vs attrition) 1.15 vs 0.89, rank-biserial r = -0.18 [-0.23, -0.13], Holm p < 0.001.
  - Problems solved per month: published (unmatched means) 13.7 vs 7.86; corrected (matched medians,
    active vs attrition) 8.80 vs 5.35, rank-biserial r = -0.28 [-0.33, -0.23], Holm p < 0.001.
  - Struggle time (min): published (unmatched means) 2,646 vs 3,059; corrected (matched medians,
    active vs attrition) 587 vs 424, rank-biserial r = -0.05 [-0.10, 0.00], Holm p = 0.191.
  - Math solve rate: published (unmatched means) 0.54 vs 0.47; corrected (matched medians, active vs
    attrition) 0.50 vs 0.46, rank-biserial r = -0.13 [-0.18, -0.08], Holm p < 0.001.
  - Data-structure solve rate: published (unmatched means) 0.48 vs 0.40; corrected (matched medians,
    active vs attrition) 0.43 vs 0.40, rank-biserial r = -0.10 [-0.15, -0.05], Holm p = 0.001.
  - Activity-decay flag: published 29.8% vs 45.0%; corrected 32.7% vs 45.0%.
Withdrawn as a result: the 83.7 percent fall, the 15.6 percent rise in struggle time, the 42.7
percent figure, and the editorial-dependency reading of upsolving (no difference, Holm p = 0.334).
What remains is smaller and holds: lower contest and practice frequency and slightly lower topic-
level accuracy, all significant after Holm correction, with |r| <= 0.28.

1.2  Activity Trend Ratio / attrition criteria vs. burnout percentages and Fig. 7
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------

INQUIRY (verbatim): "Discrepancies between the defined Activity Trend Ratio / Attrition criteria and
the reported burnout percentages and plot distributions (e.g., Fig. 7)."

What we found. The published article describes the Activity Trend Ratio in three ways that do not
agree with one another or with the code. The Dataset 1 subsection says the recent and prior windows
are "the three and six calendar months preceding T_obs". The Feature Engineering subsection calls it
"the ratio of submissions in the recent three months to those in the prior three". The Results
define burnout as activity "falling by more than half over the final three months", and the Fig. 7
axis reads "recent 6mo / prior 6mo". The code computes none of these. It divides the submissions in
the second half of each user's own submission span (first to last retrieved submission) by those in
the first half, and flags burnout when the ratio is below 0.5. Under the calendar definition, every
attrition user, having no submissions in the last three months, would have a ratio of 0, which Fig.
7 plainly does not show; this is the discrepancy the Desk observed. Fig. 7 also paired a box plot of
the raw ratio (t-test p = 0.610 on a heavy-tailed variable whose mean was higher for attrition
users, 3.91 vs 3.30) with bars of the flag, from the unmatched cohort.

What we changed. The implemented definition is now stated once (Feature Engineering subsection) and
used consistently in the text, the figure and the code, including the fallback value of 1 when the
first half is empty. On the matched cohort the flag occurs in 45.0% vs 32.7% of users (difference
12.3 percentage points, 95% CI 7.9-16.7; new Fig. 3, with Wilson intervals). We also state that, for
inactive users, the second half of the span ends at the final submission, so part of this difference
follows from the label definition. For that reason the ratio is reported but not modelled. The
attrition criterion is stated precisely: last submission at least three months before T_obs, and
peak rating below 1500. We also correct a factual slip: 1500 is not "the Codeforces Specialist
boundary" (the Specialist band is 1400-1599), so it is described as a study-specific cut-off.

1.3  Inconsistent data-leakage audit between contest and problem-solving variables
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------

INQUIRY (verbatim): "Inconsistent application of the data leakage audit between contest and problem-
solving variables."

What we found. The published audit removed contests per month (reason: a denominator "anchored to
T_obs") but kept problems solved per month. Table V describes both as "divided by account tenure".
In the code, both are in fact divided by the same submission span, so they share a single
denominator. The audit also kept the Intensity Ratio, which is built from both. The rule was
therefore applied to the contest variable but not to its problem-solving counterparts. Table V also
misdescribed post-drop recovery as "submissions in the 30 days after a rating fall"; the code
computes the mean rating change in the contest after a drop. The published Eq. (1) for the Intensity
Ratio does match the code.

What we changed. One rule is now applied throughout: every feature whose value depends on the length
or end of the observed activity window is removed (account age, both monthly rates, the trend
ratio). The Intensity Ratio is replaced by its exactly window-invariant form, problems solved per
rated contest; the span cancels between numerator and denominator (Spearman rho = 0.989). Table VI
(Appendix A) gives every definition as implemented, with the reason for each exclusion. Three
specifications are reported side by side (Table IV, Fig. 6):
  - B, 12 features: best repeated-CV ROC-AUC 0.797 +/- 0.024 (Soft-Voting Ensemble); Random Forest
    0.786.
  - C, published audit (9): best repeated-CV ROC-AUC 0.660 +/- 0.028 (Soft-Voting Ensemble); Random
    Forest 0.657.
  - D, consistent audit (8) [primary]: best repeated-CV ROC-AUC 0.622 +/- 0.026 (Soft-Voting
    Ensemble); Random Forest 0.620.
  - D without the data-quality rule: best repeated-CV ROC-AUC 0.663 +/- 0.028 (Random Forest).

1.4  Confounding survey item mappings (Q11/Q33; break-history timelines)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

INQUIRY (verbatim): "Confounding survey item mappings (e.g., upsolving Q11/Q33 alignment and break
history timelines)."

What we found. The Desk is right on both. The Dataset 2 subsection and Appendix B aligned Q11
(active: post-contest upsolving, always/sometimes/rarely/never) with Q33 (stopped: practice habit in
the month before quitting) via regular->sometimes, became irregular->rarely, stopped
completely->never. These are different behaviors, and the mapping puts even "regular" practice below
the top active answer. The resulting chi-square (23.18, Table II), its 35.5 percent feature
importance (Fig. 10), and the "behavioral half" of the Skill-Application Paradox all reflect the
coding, not behavior. Q27 (a break in the last three months) and Q36 (a break at any point in the CP
journey) refer to different periods, so combining them does not create a comparable variable.

What we changed. Only items asked identically of everyone (Q3, Q4, Q5) or worded in parallel
(Q14/Q37, Q22/Q35, Q28/Q34, with a recall caveat) enter tests or models (Dataset 2 subsection). Q11,
Q33, Q27, Q36 and Q39 are reported within their branch only. The upsolving result, Fig. 10 and the
paradox as a finding are withdrawn, and Appendix B now states which items are and are not aligned.

1.5  Chi-square statistics (Table II) and conflicting conclusions between the layers
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

INQUIRY (verbatim): "Unreproducible Chi-square test statistics (Table II) and conflicting
conclusions between Codeforces logs and survey data layers."

What we found. Table II was produced with scipy's chi2_contingency at its default settings, which
apply Yates' continuity correction to 2x2 tables only, and the paper did not say so. Anyone
computing the ordinary Pearson statistic therefore obtains different values: mentor support 2.221
(as published, Yates) vs 3.148 (Pearson), prior consideration of quitting 1.478 vs 2.309, prior
experience 0.215 vs 0.532, and long break 0.106 vs 0.360. Several tables also have expected counts
below 5, where asymptotic p-values are unreliable. The upsolving and long-break rows rest on the
non-comparable items described in 1.4. On the conclusions: The Discussion said the two layers
"return findings that point the same way" on practice between contests. Once the upsolving item is
set aside, they do not. In the platform data, attrition users were slightly less accurate, whereas
in the survey, students who stopped rated themselves slightly higher.

What we changed. The new Table II reports the six comparable items (n = 64) with Pearson chi-square
without continuity correction (stated explicitly), exact p-values (Fisher for 2x2, Monte-Carlo with
10,000 shuffles otherwise), effect sizes with confidence intervals, and Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg
adjustments. No survey difference survives; the smallest Holm-adjusted p is 0.161 (mathematics self-
rating, 3.93 vs 3.41). The Discussion now states that the two analyses neither confirm each other
nor point the same way, and treats them as separate evidence.

1.6  Below-baseline performance; training errors shown as Early Warning System signatures
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

INQUIRY (verbatim): "Model performance falling below trivial baseline classifiers, along with
classifier training errors presented as "Early Warning System" signatures."

What we found. On a balanced cohort, always predicting attrition yields F1 = 2/3. Under the
published audited Specification (C), seven of nine classifiers fall below that value, and the
published F1 of 0.682 for Random Forest is only marginally above it. Survey F1 values should
likewise have been read against 0.792, the F1 of always predicting the majority class (42 of 64).
The Early Warning System scored the same 22 active students on whom the model was trained. Even out
of fold, an active student scored as high risk is a misclassification of a known label, and the
Results described those misclassifications as the "Skill-Application Paradox signature". That
reading was not justified. The tiers also depend on library version: re-running the deposited code
gives 5/8/9 instead of the published 6/10/6.

What we changed. Every model table now includes the always-attrition and random baselines. ROC-AUC
(chance = 0.5) is the primary metric, alongside F1 and balanced accuracy with per-fold SD (5x
repeated 5-fold CV) and bootstrap test intervals. A label-permutation test (200 permutations) gives
p = 0.005 for both Random Forest and logistic regression. The behavioral signal is therefore real
but modest, and the manuscript says plainly that no classifier exceeds the trivial F1. On comparable
items the survey classifier reaches a repeated-CV ROC-AUC of at most 0.630 (permutation p = 0.065)
and is not claimed. The Early Warning System is withdrawn, and its section now explains why.

3.2  Sampling frame, seeds and T_obs
------------------------------------
  - Sampling frame (Dataset 1 subsection): the list of rated users from the Codeforces API
    (user.ratedList, activeOnly = false); a pool of 26,000 handles stratified by current rating
    band, proportional with a minimum of 50 per band. Collection gate: at least 5 rated contests,
    current rating 1-2399, at least one accepted submission. 6,927 accounts in the released file;
    every later stage is in Table I. Counts removed before the file are produced from the raw
    records by r0.
  - Seeds: 42 for handle sampling, undersampling, data splits, cross-validation folds, models,
    bootstraps and permutations. Library versions are pinned.
  - T_obs: the published article describes T_obs as "the date on which the API crawl completed". In
    the code it is the moment the feature file was built, and that moment was not recorded It falls
    in June 2026, before 24 June 2026, when the first figures were produced from the file. A later
    re-execution of the notebook on the same cached records shows 5,600/1,327 instead of the file's
    5,603/1,324, consistent with three accounts crossing the three-month threshold between runs. The
    rebuild script r0 therefore takes T_obs as an explicit argument, and it also provides a fixed-
    window rebuild in which all features use activity before T_obs minus three months only.

3.3  Ethics approval dates and institutional scope
--------------------------------------------------
  - The published article says the study was approved "on 17 March 2026". That is the date of the
    approval letter (REC/MU/2026/007, enclosed). The letter itself states that approval was given by
    resolution of the Research Ethics Committee's meeting of 24 February 2026.
  - Survey responses were collected from 6 March to 2 April 2026, after the 24 February resolution.
    Stating only the letter date made it appear that 84 of the 96 responses preceded approval. The
    corrected manuscript states both dates and the collection period (Dataset 2 subsection).
  - Scope: approval was granted by the authors' institution for the project as a whole. The survey
    was an anonymous, voluntary online form for computer science students. It recorded no name,
    student ID, e-mail or institution, and no institutional records were accessed. Because
    institution was not recorded, the corrected manuscript no longer calls the survey "multi-
    institutional", no longer gives a number of universities, and reports no per-institution
    figures. The consent statement on the form's landing page is described in the Dataset 2

  - Codeforces: public API endpoints only, at a rate within the platform's call limits; handles are
    pseudonymised with a salted hash in every released file, and accounts are never linked to survey
    respondents.
