Predicting Student Attrition in Competitive Programming: A Large-Scale Study Integrating Survey Insights and Global Behavioral Logs

Azuad Islam Ruhan, Golam Mostofa Naeem, Rakibul Islam Rafi, Sherin Afrin Mim, Nazira Bani Opi, Dewan Fahad Chowdhury, and Md. Rejaul Korim Sadi

Corrected version prepared in response to the AJSE Editorial Desk inquiry of 3 November 2026 (Paper ID 1999). All numerical results were regenerated from the deposited data and code; each change is listed in the accompanying response.

Abstract—Competitive programming develops algorithmic reasoning in computer science students, yet many withdraw after stalled progress or thin mentorship, and this attrition has not been studied as a behavioral phenomenon. We report two independent analyses: platform activity logs and a local student survey. From 6,927 Codeforces accounts, we removed 880 with incomplete submission histories and formed a balanced cohort of 1,816 users, half persistently active and half withdrawn, matched on peak rating so that disengagement is not confounded with skill. Every feature whose value depends on the length or end of the observation window was removed to prevent temporal leakage. Compared with active users of the same rating range, users who withdrew entered fewer rated contests (median 0.89 versus 1.15 per month), solved fewer problems per month (5.35 versus 8.80), and were slightly less accurate on mathematics and data-structure problems; all of these effects were small (rank-biserial |r| ≤ 0.28). The best classifier reached a cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.622 (random forest 0.620, permutation p = 0.005). This signal is above chance but modest: no model exceeded the F1 score of a trivial baseline that predicts attrition for every user. In the survey (96 responses, 64 analyzed), stalled progress was the most frequent reason for stopping (12 of 51). Students who stopped rated their mathematics ability higher than active peers (3.93 versus 3.41), but the difference does not survive correction for multiple testing, so the Skill-Application Paradox is stated as a hypothesis, not a finding.

Index Terms—Attrition prediction, competitive programming, educational data mining, machine learning, survey analytics

I. INTRODUCTION

Competitive programming (CP) has become one of the more reliable extra-curricular routes for building algorithmic reasoning in computer science students [1], [2]. Codeforces, LeetCode, and AtCoder all supply the same basic ingredients: immediate verdicts, timed contests, and a public leaderboard against which progress can be measured [3]. What separates this from a graded laboratory assignment is the absence of scaffolding. Problems arrive without hints, under fixed time and memory budgets, and the student has to decide on their own what to try next. Employers value exactly that ability [4].

The difficulty is staying with it. A large share of undergraduates begin with real enthusiasm and stop within months, a pattern also documented for dropout from introductory programming courses [5]. Computing education research has linked this pattern to swings in programming self-efficacy, a sense that progress has stalled, social isolation, and pressure from coursework [6], [7]. Dropout itself is well trodden ground in educational data mining: institutional models have been built on grade records, attendance, and clickstreams from massive open online courses [8], [9]. Competitive programming sits outside all of that. It happens off the transcript, runs on voluntary effort, and has attracted very little empirical attention [10].

Our motivation is practical. Programming clubs and faculty mentors have no systematic way of noticing that a student is drifting until the student has gone. If that drift could be detected while the student is still submitting, there would be room to act: a conversation, a pairing with a senior member, a structured review session after the next contest.

We therefore built a two-layer study and framed it around three questions. RQ1 asks which reasons students themselves select, from a closed list, for discontinuing competitive programming. RQ2 asks whether behavioral proxies drawn from Codeforces activity separate active users from true attrition users once the two groups are matched on peak rating. RQ3 asks whether models trained on Codeforces behavior or on psychographic survey responses can identify attrition better than trivial baselines.

The paper makes four contributions. First, we build a rating-matched Codeforces cohort with a documented stage-by-stage flow and an explicit data-quality rule. Second, we introduce two domain-specific interaction features, the Intensity Ratio and the Editorial Dependency Index, and report how they behave once temporal leakage is removed. Third, we benchmark nine classifiers against trivial baselines with per-fold uncertainty and a permutation test. In doing so, we quantify how much apparent signal in this kind of study comes from skill confounding, timing leakage and incomplete platform histories. Fourth, we report an exploratory survey analysis restricted to items that are comparable across questionnaire branches, together with the full instrument, data and code.

II. RELATED WORK

A. Educational Data Mining and Student Dropout

Work on student retention has shifted over two decades. Early models predicted degree completion from demographics and first-year marks [8], [11]. Attention later moved to dynamic behavior, submission patterns and forum participation, driven by the data online courses made available [9]. Random Forest [12], XGBoost [13] and LightGBM [14] are now the default comparison points for tabular educational prediction.

B. Programming Platform Telemetry

Studies of online judges show that granular submission logs carry information about cognitive state: compilation error frequency, inter-submission gaps and wrong-answer rates have been treated as proxies for frustration and learning difficulty [10], [15]. Cognitive load theory gives a reason to take long struggle times seriously, since past a point repeated failure signals overload rather than productive effort [16]. Most of this work sits in structured assignments with a fixed deadline; voluntary competitive participation behaves differently, and disengagement plays out over weeks.

C. Psychographic and Social Factors

Motivational orientation and peer support are established predictors of persistence in computing [17], [18]. Competitive environments complicate the picture. Adoption of programming contests has been studied as a diffusion process driven by peer influence and perceived benefit [19], which implies the same social channels can work in reverse when progress stalls. Educational data mining on introductory programming has also shown that behavioral traces predict failure earlier than static background variables [20]; our design follows that logic into the voluntary setting.

D. The Integration Gap

We are not aware of any prior study pairing competitive programming platform logs with psychographic survey responses. Telemetry is objective but silent on why a user stopped; surveys reach the reasoning yet depend on recall, and both raise questions of student agency and data ethics [22]. In this study the two sources share no subjects, features or labels, so we report them side by side rather than combining them.

A. Dataset 1: Codeforces Behavioral Logs

Submission histories, contest records, and profile metadata were pulled from the public Codeforces API. The sampling frame was the platform's list of rated users (user.ratedList, activeOnly = false), from which a pool of 26,000 handles was drawn, stratified by current rating band in proportion to band size with at least 50 per band (seed 42). The 6,927 accounts that passed the collection gate described below form the released feature file.

All features are computed from each account's retrieved history at a single observation time, T_(obs): the moment the feature file was built, in June 2026, before 24 June 2026. The original notebook did not record the exact time, so the deposited rebuild script now takes T_(obs) as an explicit argument. Account age and months since the last submission are measured to T_(obs), and the three-month inactivity criterion in the attrition label is measured backward from T_(obs). The per-month rates and the Activity Trend Ratio, by contrast, are computed over each account's own submission span, from its first to its last retrieved submission, not over calendar windows before T_(obs). The published version of this paragraph stated otherwise, and the Feature Engineering subsection now gives the definitions as implemented.

Accounts were retrieved only if they had at least five rated contests, a current rating between 1 and 2399, and at least one accepted submission. These criteria were enforced at collection, so all 6,927 accounts satisfy them. Peak rating below 2400 was applied afterwards and removed 35 accounts. Both modeling classes are restricted to peak ratings below 1500, so this step does not affect the modeling set.

Every rated contest requires at least one submission, so an account with fewer retrieved submissions than rated contests has an incomplete history. We excluded such accounts, together with accounts showing more than 15 rated contests per month of submission span (the highest value among inactive accounts was 9). This removed 880 accounts, all of them active at T_(obs). Left in, they would have inflated the apparent contrast between active and inactive users, because their submission-derived features are computed from truncated histories.

Inactive users fall into two very different groups. An account that had gone three consecutive months without a submission and had never exceeded 1500 was labeled True Attrition. The cut-off of 1500 is a study choice that lies inside the Codeforces Specialist band (1400–1599); it is not a platform rank boundary. The reasoning is that a user who never progressed beyond that level is unlikely to have left because they achieved what they came for. Accounts that went quiet at 1500 or above were labeled Intentional Exits and dropped, since career transitions are the more plausible explanation there.

Our first pass compared true attrition users against a random sample of all active users. That comparison is confounded, because the attrition class is capped at 1500 by construction whereas the active class is not, and any classifier trained on it can separate the groups partly by inferring skill level rather than disengagement. To isolate disengagement from ability we restricted the active pool to accounts that also peaked below 1500 and drew the 1:1 sample from within that restricted pool. There is no shortage of candidates: 3,421 active accounts fall below the threshold after the data-quality check against 908 true attrition cases. Undersampling with a fixed seed gives a balanced set of 1,816 users matched on peak rating; random undersampling is the simplest of the standard remedies for class imbalance and avoids the synthetic-sample artifacts of generative alternatives [31]. B. Dataset 2: Student Survey

The study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the authors' institution, Metropolitan University, Sylhet (approval no. REC/MU/2026/007). Approval was given by resolution of the Committee's meeting on 24 February 2026, and the approval letter was issued on 17 March 2026. Responses were collected between 6 March and 2 April 2026. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. The questionnaire collected no names, student identifiers, email addresses or institutional affiliations, and responses cannot be traced to individuals. The landing page stated the purpose of the study and that responses would be used for academic research. It also stated that submission constituted consent, and respondents could close the form at any point without submitting. No incentive was offered. The Codeforces data comes from the platform's public API and covers only information users have chosen to make public. Account handles are pseudonymized with a salted hash in every released file, all published statistics are aggregates, and no attempt was made to link accounts to survey respondents.

We distributed a structured survey online to computer science students at universities in Bangladesh and collected 96 responses. The form did not record institution, so we report no breakdown by institution. Respondents sorted into three groups: currently active in CP (22), previously active but stopped (51), and never started (23). The 23 who never started were set aside.

The 51 who had stopped were not a single population. Nine described a deliberate move into web development or machine learning, which is an intentional exit and not what we set out to model, so they were excluded. The remaining 42, who cited stalled ratings, burnout, absent mentorship, or academic pressure, were labeled true attrition. The modeling set is therefore 22 active and 42 true attrition students, n = 64, and every test and model reported below uses that set.

The questionnaire branches by status, so active and stopped respondents answered different item sets. A feature drawn from one branch alone is perfectly confounded with the outcome, and so is a feature that pairs items measuring different things. We therefore use only items that are asked identically of everyone (Q3, Q4, Q5) or worded in parallel across branches: data structure understanding (Q14 and Q37), mentor support (Q22 and Q35) and prior consideration of quitting (Q28 and Q34). The last three carry a recall caveat for the stopped group. Two pairings used in the published version are not valid and are no longer used for any between-group test or model. Q11 asks about post-contest upsolving, whereas Q33 asks about general practice habit in the month before quitting. Q27 asks about breaks in the last three months, whereas Q36 asks about breaks over the whole CP journey. Peer circle density (Q39) was asked only of stopped respondents and is dropped. Items that cannot be compared are reported within their branch only.

C. Statistical Testing

For the Codeforces cohort, whose features are heavy-tailed, we report medians and interquartile ranges, compare groups with the two-sided Mann–Whitney U test [33], and give the rank-biserial correlation with a 2,000-sample percentile bootstrap 95 percent confidence interval [28]. Proportions are reported with Wilson intervals [34]. For the survey we compare the two 1–5 ratings with Welch's t-test [25], chosen over the pooled-variance form because the groups are unequal in size, and report Cohen's d [26] with a confidence interval. Categorical items use Pearson's chi-square without continuity correction, with exact p-values: Fisher's exact test for 2×2 tables, and a Monte-Carlo permutation test with 10,000 shuffles otherwise, because several expected counts fall below 5. Cramér's V [27] is given with a bootstrap confidence interval.

Because we run six survey tests on the same sample, the conventional 0.05 threshold no longer means what it does for a single test. Across six independent tests of features that do not differ, the chance of at least one landing under 0.05 by accident is roughly 26 percent. We therefore report Holm's sequentially rejective procedure [32], which controls the family-wise error rate like the Bonferroni correction [23] but is uniformly more powerful, and Benjamini-Hochberg [24], which controls the false discovery rate at 5 percent. The same Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg corrections are applied across the eleven Codeforces comparisons. A feature that passes neither correction is a directional observation, not a finding.

D. Feature Engineering

Twelve features were derived from the raw API responses. Six are direct behavioral proxies: math solve rate, data structure solve rate, upsolves per contest, mean struggle minutes, activity trend ratio, and post-drop recovery. All models use the raw values of these features. Earlier versions also showed percentile-binned 1 to 5 scores in descriptive charts, and those charts are not used here. Table VI (Appendix A) gives the definition of every feature as implemented.

Two further features are interactions we constructed for this problem. The Intensity Ratio asks how much solving a user does relative to how often they compete:

IR_(i) = P_(month,i) / (C_(month,i) + e) (1)

where P_month is problems solved per month, C_month is contests entered per month, and e = 0.1 guards the denominator against users with no recent contest activity. A high value means the user is solving a great deal relative to how much they compete, which is the pattern we associate with self-directed practice. Because both monthly rates share the same denominator, the length of the user's submission span, that span cancels and IR approximately equals problems solved per rated contest. The audited specification therefore uses the exactly window-invariant form SPC_(i) = S_(i)/C_(i), where S_(i) is distinct problems solved and C_(i) rated contests (Spearman ρ = 0.989 with IR).

The Editorial Dependency Index encodes a suspicion we had going in, that attrition-bound users upsolve often but without much independent effort:

EDI_(i) = U_(i) × ΔT_(struggle,i) (2)

where U_(i) is upsolving frequency per contest (accepted practice-mode submissions to contests the user entered) and ΔT_(struggle) is the mean time to acceptance, itself defined as the mean of T_(AC) minus T_(first) over all accepted problems. A high EDI next to low contest frequency is the pattern the index was designed to detect.

Decay in activity is captured by the Activity Trend Ratio: the number of submissions in the second half of the user's own submission span divided by the number in the first half, set to 1 when the first half is empty. An account is flagged for activity decay when this ratio falls below 0.5. Three columns were deliberately kept out: months since last submission encodes the label definition directly, peak rating appears in the label threshold, and current rating is not measured at a comparable point in time across the two groups.

From the survey, six features enter the tests and models: mathematics self-rating and data structure understanding, both self-rated 1 to 5; course grade in Data Structures and Algorithms; mentor support; prior consideration of quitting; and programming experience before university. Upsolving habit and long breaks are described within each branch only. Semester and cumulative grade average were available but excluded, as neither is plausibly connected to CP attrition except through variables already in the model.

E. Models and Training Protocol

Nine architectures were evaluated: logistic regression with L2 regularization, a Gini-split decision tree, Random Forest with 200 estimators (Gini impurity), k-nearest neighbors at k = 5, an RBF-kernel support vector machine, XGBoost [13], LightGBM [14], a multi-layer perceptron with hidden layers of 64 and 32 units, ReLU activations and a logistic output, and a soft-voting ensemble over the first, third, fifth and sixth of these. The ensemble averages the four members' attrition probabilities with equal weights; no weights were tuned.

P̂(y = 1 | x) = (1/4) Σ_(m=1..4) P_(m)(y = 1 | x) (3)

All preprocessing and modeling pipelines were implemented using scikit-learn [21]. Balanced class weights were used wherever the implementation supports them (all models except k-nearest neighbors and the multi-layer perceptron), with XGBoost using a scaled positive weight for the same purpose. Preprocessing was median imputation followed by standardization on numeric columns and one-hot encoding on categorical ones, fitted inside each training fold. We report two protocols. The first is the protocol of the published version: stratified five-fold cross-validation [29] on the 80 percent part of an 80/20 split, with test metrics on the held-out 20 percent. The second is stratified five-fold cross-validation repeated 5 times on the full cohort, reported as mean ± SD over 25 folds. ROC-AUC is the primary metric because it does not depend on a decision threshold. F1, balanced accuracy and recall are reported alongside, and every table includes two trivial baselines: always predicting attrition, and predicting at random in class proportion. On balanced data, the always-attrition baseline has F1 = 2/3, so F1 must be read against it. Signal is tested with a label-permutation test [35] (200 permutations), and feature importance is measured as the drop in held-out ROC-AUC under permutation (30 repeats). The random seed is 42 throughout, and the exact library versions are pinned in the deposited requirements file.

IV. RESULTS

A. Behavioral Differences in the Rating-Matched Cohort

Matching worked as intended. Peak ratings in the two classes are balanced (standardized mean difference 0.055, Kolmogorov–Smirnov D = 0.061, p = 0.071), against 0.66 and 0.282 in the unmatched comparison. Active users entered a median of 1.15 rated contests per month of activity and true attrition users 0.89 (rank-biserial r = -0.18, Holm p < 0.001). The published figure of an 83.7 percent fall (7.27 vs. 1.19 contests per month) came from the unmatched cohort. It was driven by 148 active accounts with truncated submission histories, and is withdrawn. Table III gives every behavioral comparison on the matched cohort.

B. Survey: Self-Rated Skill

Students who had stopped CP rated their mathematical ability at 3.93 out of 5, against 3.41 among students still active (Welch t = 2.31, p = 0.027, d = 0.65). Data structure understanding followed the same direction, 3.52 against 3.09, though that comparison does not reach significance (p = 0.191). Neither survives correction.

Fig. 4. Self-rated skill among active (n = 22) and true attrition (n = 42) respondents, mean with 95 percent t-interval. Neither difference survives correction for multiple comparisons.

The published version called this pattern, together with weaker practice among students who stopped, the Skill-Application Paradox. Its behavioral half rested on the upsolving item. That item paired a post-contest upsolving question for active students (Q11) with a general practice-habit question about the month before quitting for stopped students (Q33), under a mapping that placed even regular practice below the top active category. The resulting group difference reflects the pairing rather than behavior, and it is withdrawn.

What remains is the confidence half, and it is directional only: students who stopped rated their own ability at least as high as the active group, without any difference being established at this sample size.

Most of the respondents who stopped had earned an A or A+ in their Data Structures and Algorithms course, so the confidence is not baseless. One interpretation is that strong coursework performance produces confidence in theoretical command, and that this confidence makes independent practice feel less necessary than it is. We state this as a hypothesis for a prospective study with identical items for all respondents, not as a finding of this one.

C. Reasons for Discontinuation

RQ1 asks what reasons students give themselves, and we should be explicit about how thin our instrument is for answering it. Q31 offered a closed list of six options with a single selection permitted; the full instrument is reproduced in Appendix B. There were no free-text responses and no interviews. What we have is a frequency distribution over researcher-supplied categories, not a qualitative analysis in any recognized sense.

Read at that level, the distribution is still informative. Stalled progress was the most common selection at 12 of 51 (23.5 percent, 95 percent CI 14.0–36.8). It was followed by an unspecified other category and a shift to other technical tracks at 9 each, lack of guidance at 8, academic pressure at 7, and burnout at 6. The intervals overlap widely. Attrition is spread across several causes rather than dominated by one. Establishing why students leave would require open-ended responses or semi-structured interviews analyzed under a stated coding procedure.

D. Classifier Performance and Leakage Audit

Before reporting performance we have to deal with a problem in our own feature set. Account age runs from registration to T_(obs), so for inactive users it includes the idle period that defines the label. Contests per month and problems solved per month are both divided by the user's submission span, so they share one denominator. The Intensity Ratio is built from both, and the Activity Trend Ratio compares the two halves of a span that, for inactive users, ends at their final submission. The audit in the published version removed account age, contests per month and the trend ratio, but kept problems solved per month and the Intensity Ratio, which depend on the same span. We now apply one rule throughout: every feature whose value depends on the length or end of the observed activity window is removed, and the Intensity Ratio is replaced by its window-invariant form SPC. Leakage is meant in the sense of Kaufman et al. [30]: information about the target enters through how the data were assembled rather than through anything the subject did.

We report three specifications, all on the data-quality-checked, rating-matched cohort. Specification B keeps all twelve features. Specification C applies the published audit (nine features). Specification D applies the consistent audit, with eight window-invariant features. The unmatched specification of the first submission is retained in the deposited code. Table IV gives Specification D in full, and Fig. 6 compares all three.

The audited models carry a real but modest signal. The best repeated-CV ROC-AUC is 0.622 ± 0.026 (Soft-Voting Ensemble), with Random Forest at 0.620. Label permutation gives p = 0.005 for Random Forest and p = 0.005 for logistic regression, the smallest values attainable with 200 permutations. The signal is nonetheless weak. Balanced accuracy does not exceed 0.590, and all 9 classifiers fall below the F1 of 0.667 achieved by always predicting attrition. Read with F1 alone and without that baseline, as in the published version, such results look stronger than they are.

The three specifications show where the higher published scores came from. With all twelve features the best AUC is 0.797. The published audit lowers it to 0.660, and the consistent audit to 0.622. Without the data-quality rule, Specification D reaches 0.663, because the truncated histories, all among active users, add separability that has nothing to do with behavior.

The tree ensembles hold up better than the linear and distance-based models once the window-dependent columns are gone. This suggests that what remains of the signal is partly interactive rather than additive. The absolute level is low, however, and we read the result as evidence that behavior before attrition differs measurably but not as a basis for individual prediction.

E. What the Branch-Alignment Correction Changed

Because the correction described above altered several headline numbers, we state its effect plainly. Two survey features were originally drawn from the stopped branch alone. Break history was taken from Q36 without pairing it to the active-branch equivalent Q27, and peer circle density from Q39, for which no active-branch item exists at all. In both cases every active respondent was missing and every stopped respondent was present, so a chi-square test recovered the branching structure of the questionnaire rather than any difference in behavior. The resulting statistics, 73.00 and 60.46, are artifacts of that separation; the first equals the sample size exactly, which is the signature of the problem.

Pairing items does not repair them if the items measure different things. Q27 (a break in the last three months) and Q36 (a break at any point in the CP journey) refer to different periods. Q11 (upsolving after contests) and Q33 (practice habit in the month before quitting) refer to different behaviors. We therefore report them only within their branch. Among active respondents, 13 of 22 reported a long break in the last three months, and Q11 answers were sometimes 12, always 6, rarely 2, never 2. Among stopped respondents, 37 of 51 reported a long break at some point, and Q33 answers were became irregular 29, regular 12, stopped completely 10. Peer circle density cannot be repaired either, since the item was never put to active respondents.

F. Feature Importance on the Behavioral Layer

Account tenure ranked first in the unaudited model. That interpretation does not hold: account age is fixed at registration and advanced by the calendar, and for inactive users it includes the idle period itself. In the matched cohort, attrition accounts are in fact older, not newer (median 33.5 vs. 19.0 months), which corrects a statement in the published version. Contest frequency, ranked second, depends on the submission span in the same way as problems solved per month.

Under the consistent audit, the window-invariant form of the Intensity Ratio, problems solved per rated contest, is the most important feature by permutation importance, followed by avg_struggle_minutes (0.035), math_solve_rate (0.029), post_drop_recovery (0.022). The balance between practice and contest participation therefore keeps its place once timing is removed, although its contribution to held-out ROC-AUC is small (0.066).

G. Early Warning System Deployment

The published version applied the survey-trained Random Forest to the 22 active respondents as a proof-of-concept Early Warning System. We withdraw it. Those 22 students were part of the training data, so an active student scored as high risk, whether in-sample or out of fold, is a misclassification of a known label. Without follow-up data there is no basis for reading those errors as at-risk profiles. In addition, the risk tiers depended on the confounded upsolving feature and changed with library version (6/10/6 published; 5/8/9 on re-execution). A prospective design that validates flags against later outcomes is left to future work.

V. DISCUSSION

A. What the Two Layers Say Together

We described this work in an earlier draft as a framework in which the two layers converge. That word claims more than the design supports, and we withdraw it. The layers share no respondents, no features and no labels. The Codeforces cohort is a global sample of anonymous accounts labeled by an inactivity proxy; the survey cohort is 64 students at Bangladeshi universities labeled by self-report. Nothing links an individual in one to an individual in the other, and no statistic computed on one constrains a statistic computed on the other.

The two analyses also do not point the same way. In the platform data, users who withdrew were slightly less accurate and less active than matched peers. In the survey, students who stopped rated their own ability slightly higher, without a significant difference. The upsolving contrast that the published version took as a shared direction rested on non-comparable survey items. Self-ratings and platform accuracy measure different things, and the survey comparison is retrospective for the stopped group. We therefore treat the two analyses as separate pieces of evidence.

B. Practical Implications

Declining contest and practice frequency is the most consistent behavioral difference we observe, and a programming club can notice it without any model. Structured post-contest review sessions and mentor pairings remain sensible responses to visible disengagement. Our results do not, however, support automated risk scoring of individual students, and the effect of such interventions would need to be tested in a controlled study.

C. Limitations

Several things constrain what we can claim. The survey modeling set is small at 64, and it is confined to Bangladeshi universities, so generalization is untested. The Codeforces labels rest on an inactivity proxy that cannot separate a permanent departure from a semester spent on coursework, and the peak-rating cut-off at 1500 is a study choice. Features summarize each account's history up to T_(obs) rather than a fixed window before the stop. A fixed-window rebuild from the raw API records is provided with the deposited code. Codeforces users are a global population, not specifically university students.

There is also a bias we cannot design away. Features such as data structure understanding, mentor support and prior consideration of quitting were reported by students who had already stopped, which means we are asking people to recall their past behavior from inside their current disengagement. If that colors their answers systematically, the separation we observe between the cohorts on self-reported measures is inflated to an unknown degree.

Finally, the questionnaire branches asked different questions, which limits the between-group comparisons that are possible. Both datasets are observational throughout, so nothing reported here supports a causal claim.

VI. CONCLUSION

This paper set out to study attrition in competitive programming using two independent sources: Codeforces behavioral logs after data-quality checks, rating matching and balancing, and an anonymous student survey in Bangladesh. In the rating-matched cohort, users who withdrew had entered fewer rated contests, solved fewer problems, and were slightly less accurate on math- and data-structure-tagged problems. These differences are significant after correction but small.

The Skill-Application Paradox is not established. Its behavioral half rested on survey items that cannot be compared across branches. Its confidence half is directional only: the mathematics self-rating gap is nominal at p = 0.027 and does not survive correction.

On the modeling side, window-invariant behavioral features predict attrition above chance but modestly, with a best repeated-CV ROC-AUC of 0.622. No model exceeds the F1 of a trivial baseline, and the survey supports no predictive model at n = 64. The difference between 0.797 with all features and 0.622 after the audit measures how much apparent predictive power in this kind of study can come from timing artifacts rather than behavior. The window-invariant form of the Intensity Ratio is the highest-ranked behavioral feature once the audit is applied.

We offer these results as an exploratory baseline rather than a finished system. The work we most want to do next is longitudinal: larger cohorts followed across universities and academic years, repository activity and club attendance folded into the feature set, and a prospective, validated early-warning procedure developed with a partner programming club. A second priority is qualitative. RQ1 asks why students leave, and a closed six-option item cannot answer that; semi-structured interviews with a subset of the cohort that stopped, coded under a stated procedure with inter-coder reliability reported, would replace the frequency distribution we offer here with something that genuinely addresses the question.
